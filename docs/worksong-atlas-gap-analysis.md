# Worksong Atlas: gap analysis and add list

Prepared 28 August 2026 against https://movingmusic.works/atlas-regions/ (92 traditions, 58 places).

## How to read this document

Each entry follows your atlas format: tradition, anchor place, movement tag, then a one-line rationale and a source. Movement tags use your existing eight: Hauling and heaving, Rowing and paddling, Animal-directed, Communal work party, Solo pacing, Signaling, Pounding and hammering, Devotional and ritual. You assign the state tag (People at work / sharing songs / in concert) yourself, since that depends on the video you pick.

Confidence levels:

- Verified: a scholarly or heritage source was confirmed during this research pass.
- Known: standard in the ethnomusicology literature; no source was re-checked this pass.
- Verify: plausible and widely reported, but you should confirm a source before adding.

Tiers:

- Tier 1: add first. A missing country or a major, well-documented genre that fills a structural hole.
- Tier 2: deepen countries you already have, or add secondary genres.
- Tier 3: borderline cases (after-work singing, ritual songs about work, revivals) or entries that need verification.

## Tier 1: add these first

### Africa

1. Somali hees hawleed (work songs)
   Hargeisa and Berbera, Somaliland. Animal-directed; Solo pacing; Hauling and heaving.
   Camel-watering, milking, mat-weaving and grain-pounding songs form a named genre (hees hawleed) in Somali poetics; Berbera's Geelle dockworkers still compose them while loading ships. Verified. Sources: Andrzejewski and Lewis, Somali Poetry (Oxford 1964); Kapteijns and Omar, Women's Voices in a Man's World (1999); Said Ahmed Warsama, Hees hawleeddo: Chansons de travail somaliennes (Djibouti 1992).

2. Dinka ox songs and cattle-camp songs
   Abyei and Bor, South Sudan. Animal-directed.
   Men compose praise songs for their personal oxen and sing them in the cattle camp; the genre anchors Dinka identity. Verified. Sources: Francis Mading Deng, The Dinka and Their Songs (Clarendon 1973); Angela Impey, Journal of African Cultural Studies 25(2), 2013.

3. Bamana and Senufo hoeing songs and champion-farmer contests
   Ségou region, Mali, and Korhogo, Côte d'Ivoire. Communal work party.
   Teams hoe to balafon and drum rhythms while women sing to push the champion cultivators; the ciwara headdress complex celebrates the same labor. Verified. Sources: Stephen Wooten, "Antelope Headdresses and Champion Farmers," African Arts 33(2), 2000; Anita Glaze, Art and Death in a Senufo Village (1981).

4. Ganda canoe paddling songs, Lake Victoria
   Ggaba and Entebbe, Uganda. Rowing and paddling.
   Transcribed canoe songs sung to keep paddling rhythm exist from the 1890s onward. Verified. Sources: J. F. Cunningham transcriptions in H. Johnston, The Uganda Protectorate (1902); Trowell and Wachsmann, Tribal Crafts of Uganda (1953).

5. Aka polyphonic hunting songs
   Lobaye, Central African Republic. Signaling; Communal work party.
   Distinct from the Mbuti net-hunting entry you already have (DRC); the Aka repertoire is a UNESCO Representative List element (2003) and the subject of Simha Arom's foundational recordings. Known. Sources: Arom, Polyphonies et polyrythmies instrumentales d'Afrique centrale (1985); UNESCO ICH "Polyphonic singing of the Aka Pygmies."

6. Hausa women's grinding and pounding songs (waƙoƙin niƙa)
   Kano, Nigeria. Pounding and hammering; Solo pacing.
   Women's songs at the grinding stone and mortar are a recognized oral-literature genre in Hausa studies. Known. Sources: Graham Furniss, Poetry, Prose and Popular Culture in Hausa (1996); Beverly Mack, Muslim Women Sing (2004).

7. Mchakamchaka (group jogging songs)
   Dodoma, central Tanzania (schoolchildren jogging to school; also JKT camps). Communal work party.
   Kids jog to school in a group, singing call-and-response to keep the pace together; the group is the point, so tag it the same way you tag the US military cadence entry, not as solo pacing. Verified. Sources: your own worksongs.org "About Mchaka" page and 2015 mchakamchaka chant post; JamiiForums and Ruhuwiko school-era song lists; JKT (Jeshi la Kujenga Taifa) performances.

### Middle East, Caucasus and Central Asia

8. Kyrgyz bekbekei, shyryldang and op maida
   Naryn and Osh, Kyrgyzstan. Animal-directed; Signaling.
   Bekbekei is sung by women guarding sheep from wolves at night; shyryldang by herders leading horse herds to pasture; op maida to horses treading grain on the threshing floor. Verified. Source: János Sipos, Kyrgyz Folksongs (Hungarian Academy of Sciences, 2014).

9. Uzbek and Tajik farming-cycle songs
   Fergana Valley and Kashkadarya, Uzbekistan. Animal-directed; Communal work party; Solo pacing.
   Named genres cover the full cycle: Qo'sh haydash (first furrow), Mayda (threshing), Yorg'uchoq (women's hand-mill), Ho'sh-ho'sh (milking calls). Verified. Source: National ICH inventory of Uzbekistan (ich.uz), "Ritual songs."

10. Coaxing ritual for camels
   Gobi provinces, Mongolia. Animal-directed; Devotional and ritual.
   A herder sings to persuade a camel to accept a rejected calf; separate from generic herding songs and on UNESCO's Urgent Safeguarding List (2015). Known. Source: UNESCO ICH "Coaxing ritual for camels."

11. Bushehr neymeh (sailors' and fishers' work songs)
    Bushehr, Iran. Hauling and heaving.
    Persian Gulf sea work songs that parallel Bahrain's fijiri on the Iranian shore; Iran currently has no entry. Verified. Sources: Lian Ensemble and Shanbehzadeh Ensemble documentation; Bristol University Iranian folk music survey (2024).

12. Caspian women's rice-paddy songs
    Rasht (Gilan) and Sari (Mazandaran), Iran. Communal work party.
    Women dominate rice transplanting on the Caspian littoral and sing while doing it; texts are published with linguistic analysis. Verified. Source: Habib and Maryam Borjian, "Ethno-linguistic materials from rural Mazandaran," Iran and the Caucasus 11 (2007).

13. Georgian work-song set beyond naduri and orovela
    Odoia (Samegrelo, weeding maize), Urmuli (Kartli, ox-cart driver), Elesa (Guria, hauling), Mtibluri (Tusheti and Pshavi, mowing), Namgluri (reaping). Communal work party; Animal-directed; Hauling and heaving; Solo pacing.
    Each is a named genre in Georgian dialect classification; Urmuli and Elesa exist in 1907 wax-cylinder recordings. Verified. Sources: International Research Center for Traditional Polyphony (polyphony.ge); National Archives of Georgia.

### South Asia

14. Bhojpuri women's work-song cycle: jantsar, ropani, sohani, nirwahi
    Ballia and Ghazipur, eastern Uttar Pradesh; Bihar. Solo pacing; Communal work party.
    Jantsar (songs of the hand-mill) is the best-studied women's labor genre in North India; ropani (transplanting), sohani (weeding) and nirwahi (late weeding) follow the crop cycle. Verified. Sources: Smita Tewari Jassal, "Bhojpuri songs, women's work and social control," Journal of Peasant Studies 30(2), 2003; Jassal, Unearthing Gender (Duke 2012).

15. Hurkiya Bol (Hudkiya Baul)
    Pithoragarh and Almora, Kumaon, Uttarakhand. Communal work party.
    A hurka-drum bard sings ballads while lines of women transplant paddy; the field becomes a performance. Verified. Sources: Shukla and Purohit, Language in India 12 (2012); Sahapedia, "Hurkiyas: The Guardians of Oral Traditions of Kumaon."

16. Vanchipattu (snake-boat rowing songs)
    Kuttanad and Aranmula, Kerala. Rowing and paddling.
    An asan leads, up to 64 oarsmen answer, and the stroke follows the song; a rowing tradition with an 18th-century literary canon. Verified. Sources: Vijayan Kannampilly, "Vanchipattu: Songs of the Boat Race in Kerala," in Water (Pearson 2009); Nehru Trophy Boat Race documentation.

17. Sri Lankan kavi cycle beyond goyam kavi
    Pal kavi (watch-hut songs of chena farmers), Karatta kavi (bullock-cart drivers), Paru kavi (river boatmen), Pathal kavi (gem-pit miners), Kamath kavi (threshing floor), Nelum kavi (transplanting). Solo pacing; Animal-directed; Rowing and paddling; Pounding and hammering.
    Each is a named jana kavi genre; pathal kavi (gem miners) is rare in the world literature. Verified (popular and museum sources); scholarly confirmation: Verify.

### East Asia

18. Chinese haozi families beyond Chuanjiang
    Yellow River boatmen's haozi (Lanzhou, Gansu); Shanghai port haozi (national ICH 2008); Zhoushan fishermen's haozi (Zhejiang); Northeast forest haozi (Heilongjiang timber); Bashan beier ge, porters' songs (Sichuan). Hauling and heaving; Rowing and paddling; Pounding and hammering.
    Haozi is a formal category of Chinese folk song and China's national ICH list carries dozens of regional forms; the atlas has one. Verified. Sources: Igor Chabrowski, Singing on the River: Sichuan Boatmen and Their Work Songs (Brill 2015); China national ICH list; china.org.cn "Haozi: Work Songs."

19. Bunun pasibutbut (prayer for the millet harvest)
    Taitung and Nantou, Taiwan. Devotional and ritual; Communal work party.
    Eight-part polyphony sung by men before millet sowing; a foundational recording sent to UNESCO in 1952 by Kurosawa Takatomo. Verified. Sources: Council of Indigenous Peoples, Taiwan; Hualien County Cultural Affairs Bureau registration.

20. Yaeyama yunta and jiraba
    Taketomi and Ishigaki, Okinawa. Communal work party.
    Unaccompanied peasant work songs sung in call and response by gender-divided groups, distinct from the elite sanshin repertoire; Asadoya Yunta is the most famous. Verified. Source: Matt Gillan, Songs from the Edge of Japan (Ashgate 2012).

21. Kiyari and jizuki-uta
    Tokyo (Edo) and Tochigi, Japan. Hauling and heaving; Pounding and hammering.
    Kiyari were sung by log carriers moving timber and stone and became the firemen's guild chant; jizuki-uta coordinate ground-tamping and pile-driving crews. Verified. Sources: Edo Firemanship Preservation Association; Kokusai Bunka Shinkokai, Songs of People at Work and Play (1941, reissued Arbiter Records).

22. Namdo deulnorae (Jindo rice-field songs)
    Jindo, South Jeolla, Korea. Communal work party.
    National Intangible Cultural Heritage No. 51 (1973), sung in the paddies to drum accompaniment; the flagship of Korean nodongyo classification. Verified. Sources: Korea Heritage Service inventory; UNESCO ICHCAP Korea inventory (2013).

### Southeast Asia and the Pacific

23. Ví and Giặm folk songs of Nghệ Tĩnh
    Vinh and Hà Tĩnh, Vietnam. Communal work party; Rowing and paddling.
    UNESCO Representative List (2014); sub-genres are named for the work they accompany: ví phường vải (weaving), ví phường nón (hat making), ví đò đưa (rowing). Verified. Source: UNESCO ICH 01008.

24. Phleng kiao khao (rice-harvesting songs) and phleng ruea (boat songs)
    Suphanburi and Ayutthaya, central Thailand. Communal work party; Rowing and paddling.
    Call-and-response harvest and boat songs of the central plains; Thailand has no entry. Verified. Source: Mahidol Music Journal, "Harmonising Tradition: Choralisation of Central Thai Kiao Khao Folk Songs" (2026).

25. Ivatan kalusan
    Uyugan, Batanes, Philippines. Communal work party; Rowing and paddling.
    Work songs led by a song leader in the fields, in boats and while sawing timber; disappearing and barely recorded. Verified. Source: Florentino Hornedo, Laji: An Ivatan Folk Lyric Tradition.

26. Marshallese roro (navigation and canoe-hauling chants)
    Rongelap and Majuro, Marshall Islands. Signaling; Hauling and heaving; Rowing and paddling.
    Chants recite sea marks for navigators and give strength for launching and beaching canoes; still used in the canoe revival. Verified. Source: Jack Tobin, Stories from the Marshall Islands (University of Hawai'i Press 2002); Waan Aelõñ in Majel.

27. Holehole bushi
    Waipahu and Hilo, Hawai'i. Solo pacing; Communal work party.
    Japanese immigrant women stripping cane leaves composed some 200 surviving songs; a Pacific work-song tradition of migration and labor. Verified. Source: Franklin Odo, Voices from the Canefields (Oxford 2013).

### Europe

28. Norwegian lokk, laling and kauking
    Folldal and Alvdal, Østerdalen, Norway. Animal-directed; Signaling.
    Norway is absent from the atlas despite your Synnøve Brøndbo Plassen collaboration; add the Østerdalen herding-call tradition and, separately, Norwegian opsang (sailors' hauling songs). Known.

29. Cape Breton milling frolic
    Iona and Christmas Island, Cape Breton, Nova Scotia. Hauling and heaving; Communal work party.
    The New World continuation of waulking, still held every summer; men took over the singing when it moved from work to entertainment. Verified. Sources: Heather Sparling, "Song Genres, Cultural Capital and Social Distinctions in Gaelic Cape Breton" (2006); Bret Woods, FSU PhD (2011).

30. Mallorcan tonades de feina
    Sa Pobla and Llevant, Mallorca. Solo pacing; Animal-directed; Communal work party.
    Tonades de llaurar, segar and batre: melismatic solo work songs across the Catalan-speaking Mediterranean; Samper found singers could not recall the words without doing the task. Verified. Sources: Baltasar Samper, "Les cançons de treballs agrícoles a Mallorca" (1936); Ayats, Sureda and Vicens, Les tonades de feina a Mallorca (2006).

31. Briolage and its regional variants
    Boischaut, Berry, France (also grandes in Auvergne, tiaulage in Nivernais, dariolage in Vendée, huchage in Poitou). Animal-directed.
    Ploughmen's shouted melody that keeps yoked oxen pulling; France has only chants de marins so far. Verified. Sources: Arnold Van Gennep, Manuel de folklore français; CREM-CNRS sound archives (Fernand Bordage recordings).

32. Canto dei battipali (Venetian pile drivers)
    Pellestrina and Venice, Italy. Pounding and hammering.
    Each verse marks the fall of the maul on the pile; Lomax recorded the last battipali in 1954. Verified. Sources: Alan Lomax Italian recordings (1954); Roberto Leydi, I canti popolari italiani (1973).

33. Greek ergatika tragoudia
    Chios (mastic gathering), Thrace (reaping and threshing), Kalymnos (sponge boats). Communal work party; Rowing and paddling; Solo pacing.
    Greek folk-song classification names rowing, mill, reaping, weaving, hoeing, vintage, mastic and raisin work songs; Greece has no entry. Verified (classification); individual sources: Verify. Sources: Samuel Baud-Bovy, Essai sur la chanson populaire grecque (1983); Kostas Spanos, Dimotika tragoudia tis Deskatis.

34. Welsh ox-driving songs (canu cethrain)
    St Athan, Vale of Glamorgan, Wales. Animal-directed.
    Tribannau sung to yoked oxen by the cathreiwr walking backwards in front of the team; oxen ploughed in Glamorgan until about 1880, and VRï are reviving the songs. Verified. Sources: Museum Wales folk song collection (Roy Saer); trac Wales.

### Americas

35. Colombian-Venezuelan llano work songs
    Casanare, Colombia, and Apure, Venezuela. Animal-directed; Solo pacing.
    Cantos de ordeño, arreo and cabrestero, sung solo a cappella to cattle; UNESCO Urgent Safeguarding List (2017). Verified. Source: UNESCO ICH USL/01285.

36. Jahuay (haway)
    Colta and Guamote, Chimborazo, Ecuador. Communal work party.
    A paqui leads the reaping line and the chorus answers "jahuay"; cognate with your Cusco haylli, and Ecuador's Instituto Nacional de Patrimonio Cultural has a safeguarding plan. Verified. Source: INPC, Plan de Salvaguardia del Repertorio Musical del Jahuay (2014).

37. Grenada and Carriacou boat-launching and net-hauling chanteys
    Windward, Carriacou. Hauling and heaving.
    Lomax's 1962 recordings show British shanties reworked for boat launching, boat pulling and sail hoisting by Grenadian fishing folk. Verified. Source: Alan Lomax Collection, Roll and Go: Chanteys and Sailor Songs from Grenada; Music for Work and Play: Carriacou 1962.

38. Pueblo and Hopi corn-grinding songs
    Laguna Pueblo, New Mexico, and Hopi mesas, Arizona. Pounding and hammering; Solo pacing.
    Natalie Curtis's first publication (1905) was three Laguna corn-grinding songs; complements your Diné entry. Verified. Sources: Curtis, Songs of Ancient America (1905); The Indians' Book (1907).

39. Roustabout and coonjine songs
    Paducah, Kentucky, and Memphis, Tennessee. Hauling and heaving.
    Black steamboat deckhands loaded cargo to song and a shuffling step (coonjine); Mary Wheeler is the only print collection. Verified. Sources: Mary Wheeler, Steamboatin' Days (LSU Press 1944); Charles Ipcar (Richmond, Maine), "Steamboat and Roustabout Songs."

## Tier 3: borderline, after-work, or needs verification

These are worth a note in the atlas but do not meet a strict "sung while working" test, or you should confirm a source first.

- Canto cardenche, Sapioriz, Durango, Mexico. Peons of the cotton haciendas sang it after the day's picking, not during; an after-work tradition like the forecastle song. Verified as after-work.
- Gaa odi lava, Malé, Maldives. Performed on completion of hard manual work (originally coral-stone hauling), not during it. Verified as after-work.
- Sámi joik, Kautokeino. Reindeer herders joik about animals and people, but joik is a portrait song, not a herding call; do not label it as one.
- Ojkanje and ganga, Dalmatian hinterland and Herzegovina. Shepherds' outdoor singing, UNESCO-listed (ojkanje 2010), but not task-coordinated.
- Hungarian arató dalok and pásztordalok, Great Plain. Verify whether the harvest songs were sung in the field or at the harvest feast.
- Quebec chansons de chantier, Mauricie. Logging-camp songs, mostly evening repertoire.
- Kabyle women's grinding-mill songs, Tizi Ouzou, Algeria. Verify; Mouloud Mammeri and Tassadit Yacine are the starting points.
- Bhutanese rammed-earth building songs, Paro and Punakha. Women pound earth walls in rhythm to song; parallels your Tibetan arga entry. Verify.
- Oman fann al-bahr (sea songs), Sur. Verify.
- Iraqi Marsh Arab mashhuf paddling songs, Chibayish. Verify.
- Yemeni well-irrigation and harvest songs, Sana'a highlands. Verify.
- Newfoundland sealing and fishing songs. Mostly narrative, not work-coordinated.
- Australian Aboriginal and Torres Strait traditions. No documented task-coordination genre surfaced; leave until a source appears.

## Cross-cutting genre suggestions

These clusters cut across regions and could become filter tags or map layers.

- Street cries and vendor calls: Cries of London, Cris de Paris (Janequin 1530), voci di Napoli, pregones of Havana and Mexico City, pregões of Lisbon, Charleston and New Orleans. All fit Signaling.
- Auctioneer chants: tobacco (Virginia), cattle (Midwest), fish (Tsukiji). Signaling; Solo pacing.
- Pile driving and ground tamping: Venice battipali, Japanese jizuki-uta, Chinese dahang haozi, Tibetan arga, Bhutanese rammed earth. Pounding and hammering.
- Canoe launching and beaching: Marshall Islands, Carriacou, Bequia, Ghana, Bahamas. Hauling and heaving.
- Spinning bees and evening work sessions: Spinnstube, sedyanka, prelo, kvöldvaka, kvøldseta, trinjan, posidelki. Communal work party.
- Waterwheel and irrigation songs: shaduf (Egypt), saqiya (Nubia), Tamil water-lifting, Kurdish and Yemeni well songs. Solo pacing; Animal-directed.

## Key scholarly references for the atlas bibliography

- Ted Gioia, Work Songs (Duke 2006). The global survey; check its chapter list against the atlas.
- Karl Bücher, Arbeit und Rhythmus (1896). The founding argument that rhythm organizes labor.
- Marek Korczynski, Michael Pickering and Emma Robertson, Rhythms of Labour: Music at Work in Britain (Cambridge 2013).
- Igor Chabrowski, Singing on the River: Sichuan Boatmen and Their Work Songs (Brill 2015).
- Francis Mading Deng, The Dinka and Their Songs (Clarendon 1973).
- Smita Tewari Jassal, Unearthing Gender: Folksongs of North India (Duke 2012).
- Franklin Odo, Voices from the Canefields (Oxford 2013).
- Mary Wheeler, Steamboatin' Days (LSU Press 1944).
- János Sipos, Kyrgyz Folksongs (Hungarian Academy of Sciences 2014).
- Matt Gillan, Songs from the Edge of Japan (Ashgate 2012).
- Ayats, Sureda and Vicens, Les tonades de feina a Mallorca (2006).
- UNESCO ICH elements: Llano work songs (2017), Ví and Giặm (2014), Coaxing ritual for camels (2015), Aka polyphony (2003), Georgian polyphony (2001), Hua'er (2009).
